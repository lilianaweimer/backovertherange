# About

Back Over the Range maps places described in public-domain American travel
guides, starting with the West, with each guide's own words, and where possible notes what is
at each place now.

## Sources

Guides in the US public domain: published in 1930 or earlier, or later with no
copyright renewal on record. Railroad guides, road and highway guides, general
handbooks and travelogues. Every guide will have its own page with a link to a full
scan, usually on the Internet Archive. [See all guides](#/guides).

## How I add data

Each entry is transcribed by hand from a scan, with the guide, edition and
page. I'll use original spelling and punctuation and such. Where possible, a place's coordinates come
from the US Geological Survey's Geographic Names Information System (GNIS), the
federal government's official point for each named place, so "San Francisco"
is the same spot every time. If a place is not in GNIS for whatever reason, I'll add it as approximate using maps or whatever else I can find..

## About "today"

I won't write notes about the present for places I don't have information about. If I've been there and have either my own observations or photos, I will add those, or if I can find current information I may cite it. Any place I can't find anything about will be "unknown."

## Visiting these places

This is a record of what old books say, not a travel guide. Locations may be
approximate, and a place on the map may be on private or tribal land, closed to
the public, unsafe, or not there at all anymore. My own notes on the present may also be out of date.

I make no claim about whether any place can be visited, and take no
responsibility for anyone who goes. The "Get there" links open third-party map
apps, which may route you somewhere you should not go. Please check current conditions
and permissions yourself, and respect property and closures.

## About me

A "Visited" mark on a place means I've been there myself.

## Where it started

The project began with a volunteer 2017 inventory of engraving plates at the Denver
Public Library, which led to an undergraduate study of two books: Ernest Ingersoll's _The Crest of the Continent_ and Stanley Wood's _Over the Range to the Golden Gate._ To study them, I both traveled some of the routes either taken by Ingersoll or described by Wood and using my own observations and research, wrote my undergraduate thesis on the environmental history of the American West through that lens. Since then, though I've not gone further in formal study, I've never lost interest in the books or history. I figure: I can't be the only one who thinks it's interesting to look back at the past like this, so why not share?
